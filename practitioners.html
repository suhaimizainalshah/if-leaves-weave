For youth and case workers

Four visual tools that build one picture of a young person’s life: who their family are, who supports them, where they stand among their peers, and what has happened over time.

GenogramWho is in the family, across generations, and the patterns that repeat. EcomapThe people, groups and services around them now, and which ties help or strain. SociogramWhere they stand in a group, such as a class or youth group: who chooses whom. ChronologyWhat happened when, its source and the response, so patterns show over time.

How they fit together in an assessment

Each answers a different question, and they are strongest together. A genogram might show a pattern of loss across generations; the ecomap shows whether supports are in place now; the sociogram shows whether the young person is isolated in their class or group; and the chronology shows when concerns began and whether they cluster around particular events.

  1. Start with the genogram to understand family structure and history (McGoldrick, Gerson and Petry, 2020).
  2. Map the present with the ecomap: strong, weak and stressful ties, and which way support flows (Hartman, 1978).
  3. Look at peer relationships with a sociogram, where group information is available and appropriate (Moreno, 1934).
  4. Keep the chronology throughout: it records the sequence of events, their impact, sources and responses (Care Inspectorate, 2017).
  5. Bring them together when analysing: does a stressful tie on the ecomap match a cluster of events in the chronology? Does the genogram explain a gap in support?

Doing it with, not to

These diagrams work best completed together. Hartman wrote that the ecomap’s main value lies in its visual impact (Hartman, 1978), and seeing their world on one page can help a young person say what matters. The Ecomap’s Draw it together mode gives large controls for a tablet, so they can choose the people and the kind of each tie themselves.

What the research says

  • Genogram: a graphic way of organising the large amount of information gathered in a family assessment, widely used by family therapists and health professionals; the standard text sets out how to construct one, interview with it and interpret it (McGoldrick, Gerson and Petry, 2020).
  • Ecomap: introduced by the social worker Ann Hartman to assess families in the child welfare system (Hartman, 1978). It has been used beyond social work, in nursing and psychology, and as a research tool that can bridge practice and research (Harold, Mercier and Colarossi, 1997). The circles-of-closeness view follows Antonucci’s hierarchical mapping (Antonucci, 1986).
  • Sociogram: developed by Jacob Moreno, whose Who Shall Survive? (1934) describes sociometry, the study of relationships within groups, drawing largely on research at a reformatory, the New York Training School for Girls.
  • Chronology: the Care Inspectorate’s practice guide sets out what a good chronology contains and how it reveals patterns (Care Inspectorate, 2017). Reviews of significant cases have repeatedly highlighted the need for better recognition of patterns through chronologies.

Use these as aids to professional judgement, not as measures. The ecomap, for example, is a standard practice tool with limited psychometric study, and completing these diagrams takes time and raises privacy questions.

Good recording, and privacy

  • Everything stays on your device. Nothing is uploaded. Download a copy if you need to keep it, and store it as your organisation requires.
  • Use initials or a case reference, not full names.
  • Record facts and their source, and keep the chronology succinct: significant events, not every contact.
  • Remember people may see their records. Write as if the young person or their family will read it.
  • Follow your organisation’s policies and data-protection law, such as the UK GDPR or Singapore’s PDPA.
